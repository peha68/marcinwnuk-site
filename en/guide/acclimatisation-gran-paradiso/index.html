Acclimatising for Mont Blanc: Gran Paradiso and altitude sickness

Acclimatisation is there to test your body, gear, team and reaction to altitude before you stand below Mont Blanc. For us that test was Gran Paradiso.

Marcin Wnuk, normal route via the Goûter, June 2026

In short

  • Gran Paradiso (4,061 m) is a full mountain day, not a stroll.
  • The rocky finish needs a head for exposure. For acclimatisation, time just below the top is enough.
  • A weaker acclimatisation day does not decide your Mont Blanc result.
  • With signs of cerebral or pulmonary oedema you descend at once.

Why Gran Paradiso

Gran Paradiso is high, well placed for Mont Blanc and takes you above 4,000 m. It is not a stroll: a long climb, a glacier, altitude and a demanding finish. We roped up below the rocks. Nothing on the normal route up Mont Blanc is as technical as that last section. If you don’t feel confident there, you don’t need to stand on the very top. If you do, the reward is the view, including Mont Blanc itself.

Other options

Other teams choose the Allalinhorn above Saas-Fee, the Breithorn above Zermatt or the Aiguille du Midi. None of them is the best for everyone. Rather than looking for the easiest four-thousander, check whether a peak really helps you acclimatise, doesn’t use up too much energy and fits the logistics of the trip.

A weaker day is not a verdict

After Gran Paradiso I thought I was the weakest in the team and probably wouldn’t make it. I came up last and was very tired. Then each stage went better, and at the Vallot I was coping better than many others. Acclimatisation guarantees nothing, but without it the risk grows.

Altitude sickness

Above about 2,500 m the body gets less oxygen. Altitude sickness can affect anyone, and good fitness does not protect you. Symptoms of acute mountain sickness are headache (often the first sign), nausea, loss of appetite, more fatigue than the effort explains, dizziness and poor sleep.

Life-threatening symptoms:

  • cerebral oedema (HACE): loss of balance, unsteady walking, confusion, unusual behaviour, drowsiness,
  • pulmonary oedema (HAPE): breathlessness at rest, a cough, sometimes with frothy or pink sputum, a sudden drop in performance, blue lips.

A simple test: walk a few steps along a line, heel right in front of the other foot’s toes. If someone wobbles, take it very seriously.

Rules

  • with symptoms you don’t go higher, and if they don’t ease with rest, you go down,
  • with signs of cerebral or pulmonary oedema you descend at once, even at night, and call for help,
  • a person with symptoms never descends alone,
  • walk at your own pace, drink and eat even without appetite,
  • no alcohol or sleeping pills before summit day,
  • don’t hide symptoms from the team, and watch the others,
  • take medication only after talking to a doctor, because it does not replace descent.

Not every headache at altitude is altitude sickness. After the summit mine turned out to be a helmet that was too tight. But you only make that call once altitude can be ruled out. I am not a doctor, and these are basic rules worth knowing before the trip.

The mental side

Mental preparation includes preparing to turn back. Not reaching the summit is not a failure. Ignoring conditions, hiding symptoms and pushing on at any cost would be.
